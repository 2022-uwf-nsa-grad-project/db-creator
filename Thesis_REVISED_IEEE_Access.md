# **A Graph-Based Infrastructure for Analyzing Lateral Movement Patterns in APT Campaigns: Design, Implementation, and Baseline Characterization**

---

## **ABSTRACT**

This paper presents a scalable graph-based infrastructure for analyzing lateral movement in Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) campaigns, designed to support retrospective analysis and motivate future supervised learning approaches. The work prioritizes reproducibility, computational efficiency, and transparent methodological documentation. The system ingests 1,898,613 labeled Zeek telemetry edges from the UWF-ZeekData24 corpus into a Neo4j graph database, implementing a hybrid architecture that overcomes traditional graph database memory constraints. By exporting edges via the Awesome Procedures on Cypher (APOC) library and streaming into Polars DataFrames with lazy evaluation, the pipeline enables memory-efficient processing of multi-hop attack chains ranging from 2 to 10 hops without sampling, materializing complete chain spaces (e.g. 668 million 2-hop sequences, 198 million 3-hop sequences) on commodity hardware. The fully automated workflow orchestrates Docker container lifecycle, database refresh cycles, FastRP embedding generation via Neo4j Graph Data Science library, and synchronized artifact production (CSV summaries, high-resolution visualizations, execution logs).

## **Chapter I: Introduction**

### **I.A Background and Motivation**

The cybersecurity landscape has evolved from defending against isolated, automated malware to combating human-driven Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) campaigns. These adversaries are characterized by their patience, resources, and specific objectives, often residing within a victim's network for weeks or months. A hallmark of APT tradecraft is the blend of stealthy reconnaissance with selective lateral movement, the technique adversaries use to move through a network in search of key assets and data. They do not attack blindly; instead, they map the network topology, identify critical bridges between subnets, and pivot from one compromised host to another to reach their ultimate target.

Zeek telemetry and MITRE ATT&CK-aligned analytics have significantly improved visibility into these tactics. Defenders can now enumerate the specific techniques in play, mapping observed network flows to the ATT&CK matrix. However, this visibility has created a new problem: alert fatigue. Security Operations Centers (SOCs); centralized units that deal with security issues on an organizational and technical level; are flooded with alerts generated by reconnaissance activities. Every port scan, every service discovery attempt, and every unusual connection request generates a ticket. The critical challenge, or “triage bottleneck,” is determining which of these thousands of reconnaissance victims represents benign scanning versus nodes occupying structural positions associated with subsequent lateral movement.

Current triage methods often treat every reconnaissance alert with equal weight, leading to a misallocation of limited analyst resources. Network graphs offer a solution to this problem by exposing the structural context that adversaries exploit. In a network graph, nodes that bridge multiple subnets or hold high centrality scores are inherently more attractive to an attacker because compromising them opens up a wider array of subsequent targets. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs); deep learning models designed to process data represented as graphs; are uniquely capable of capturing these structural signatures. By aggregating information from a node's neighborhood, GNNs can learn a representation (embedding) that encodes not just the node's local activity, but its structural role within the wider network.
This research establishes a scalable infrastructure for analyzing structural patterns in APT lateral movement through retrospective analysis of a curated dataset. We demonstrate that meaningful structural signals exist and are quantifiable, providing foundational evidence and tooling for future supervised prediction systems. However, the current work prioritizes reproducibility and computational efficiency over operational deployment readiness. Methodological constraints detailed in Section 1.7; including partial temporal leakage, extreme class imbalance, and absence of supervised baselines; bound results to exploratory baseline characterization. Our contributions focus on infrastructure scalability, methodological lessons learned, and transparent limitation documentation rather than production-ready classification models.


### **I.B Problem Statement**

The core research question addressed in this work is: Do reconnaissance victims that subsequently transition into lateral movement pivots exhibit distinct structural signatures within the network graph compared to those that remain dormant?

Given the temporal communication graph derived from Zeek network logs (IP addresses as nodes, connections as edges), and a subset of nodes that have exhibited reconnaissance behavior within a specific time window, the objective is to characterize whether nodes that subsequently initiate offensive lateral movement (pivots) occupy structurally distinct positions within the graph compared to those that do not pivot.

The analytical challenges are highlighted by the characteristics of the UWF-ZeekData24 dataset, which serves as the testbed for this study. The dataset contains 57,384 labeled reconnaissance windows distributed across 357 unique IP nodes and 21 distinct subnets, underscoring the difficulty of manual triage. Of these windows, 27,214 (47.42%) eventually escalate into lateral movement, though this activity is highly concentrated and sourced from only 13 specific pivot IP addresses. While this 47.42% pivot rate is still elevated compared to typical enterprise environments (less than 1%), it is substantially lower than initially reported due to corrected window counting methodology. The median time from the initial reconnaissance event to the first initiating pivot action is merely 0.41 hours (approximately 24.6 minutes), underscoring the operational urgency of understanding pivot patterns. There is also a strong skew in the behavior of different subnets, where certain /24 blocks (143.88.3, 143.88.13, 143.88.7) are repeatedly weaponized by adversaries, serving as reliable launchpads, while others (such as 143.88.10) are subjected to extensive scanning but never transition into pivot points. Capturing this subnet-level variance is crucial for understanding APT tactics.


### **I.C Research Objectives**

To address the research question and overcome the identified challenges, this work pursues several primary objectives. First, engineer a reproducible Neo4j ingestion pipeline by building a robust data engineering pipeline capable of ingesting raw Zeek telemetry into a Neo4j graph database, meticulously preserving all relevant metadata including subnet information, precise timestamps to maintain temporal order, and MITRE ATT&CK labels. This foundation enables complex graph queries and structural analysis.

Second, generate structural node embeddings via FastRP by implementing and configuring the Fast Random Projection (FastRP) algorithm within the Neo4j Graph Data Science library to generate low-dimensional vector representations (embeddings) for each node that capture its structural position within the network. Based on these embeddings, design a similarity-based scoring workflow to quantify structural resemblance to historical pivot patterns.

Third, quantify structural differentiation of pivot nodes by rigorously evaluating whether the generated embeddings encode meaningful structural differences between reconnaissance windows that lead to pivots versus those that do not. This evaluation uses statistical hypothesis testing to assess effect sizes and significance levels, providing evidence for or against the existence of discriminative structural signals.

Fourth, develop scalable multi-hop chain analytics by extending analysis tooling beyond single-edge pivots to characterize complete attack chains spanning 2-10 hops, revealing adversary dwell times, propagation patterns, and subnet-level choke points across varying campaign depths.

Finally, document operational integration framework by translating analytical findings into concrete SOC workflows, including alert enrichment strategies, risk-tiered triage protocols, and subnet-level containment recommendations, while clearly delineating current capabilities from future predictive system requirements.


### **I.D Contributions**

This work makes several distinct contributions to the field of network security infrastructure and graph analytics methodology.

First, it delivers a scalable graph analytics infrastructure through a fully automated, production-grade pipeline (“thesis_pipeline.ipynb”) implementing a novel Neo4j + Polars hybrid architecture that overcomes traditional graph database memory constraints. The system orchestrates Docker container lifecycle, database ingestion of 1.9M edges, FastRP embedding generation, and synchronized artifact production (CSV summaries, high-resolution plots, execution logs). The architecture enables processing of complete multi-hop chain spaces (668M 2-hop, 198M 3-hop, 45M 4-hop sequences) without sampling on commodity hardware, providing a reproducible foundation for community extension.

Second, it provides memory-efficient chain construction methodology through implementation of a Polars-based lazy evaluation system with iterative self-joins and streaming collection that materializes complete n-hop attack chains (2-10 hops) without holding entire result sets in RAM. By exporting Neo4j edges via APOC and performing temporal-constrained joins at the dataframe level, the approach overcomes Cypher query limitations and eliminates arbitrary sampling constraints that bias prior work.

Third, it establishes baseline structural characterization with train/test split by demonstrating that reconnaissance windows transitioning to pivots exhibit measurably distinct FastRP embeddings compared to non-pivot windows (Cohen's d = 0.588, p < 1e-271) with proper 50/50 temporal train/test separation across 57,384 total reconnaissance windows. Mean similarity to training-set-derived prototypes for pivots (0.390) significantly exceeds non-pivots (0.242), with embeddings clustering at /24 subnet boundaries (14 unique similarity values across 21 subnets). While balanced accuracy of 0.50 limits operational utility, effect size analysis provides quantitative evidence that structural topology encodes information about subsequent lateral movement behavior, warranting supervised learning investigation.

Fourth, it documents methodological lessons and design trade-offs through comprehensive documentation of design decisions, failed approaches, and computational trade-offs that inform future work. Median-based temporal filtering enables tractable computation but introduces partial leakage for 50% of samples. Label-agnostic volume heuristics fail catastrophically (Cohen's d = -0.32), demonstrating that connection count does not correlate with structural pivot signatures. Temporal features (Burst Score: Cohen's d = 0.877) significantly outperform structural embeddings alone, suggesting ensemble approaches combining both signal types. Subnet-level embedding granularity reduces overfitting risk and aligns with SOC operational workflows.

Finally, it provides transparent limitation documentation and reproducibility through comprehensive reporting of experimental parameters, statistical tests, computational requirements, version pinning, and methodological constraints including partial temporal leakage, extreme class imbalance (94.85% pivots), and absence of supervised baselines. Section 1.7, Chapter 5 discussion, and code availability statements provide complete transparency enabling rigorous peer evaluation and community replication. All source code, configuration files, and experimental artifacts are made publicly available with detailed README documentation.


### **I.E Research Hypotheses**

The research is guided by three core hypotheses that test whether structural graph properties correlate with lateral movement behavior.

H1 (Structural Differentiation) posits that reconnaissance victims that subsequently transitioned into pivot nodes exhibit higher cosine similarity to a historical pivot prototype embedding than those that remained dormant. This hypothesis posits that there exists a distinct structural signature associated with pivoting behavior that FastRP embeddings can capture, and that this signature is consistent enough to provide measurable separation in embedding space. Note that this is a retrospective characterization hypothesis, not a prospective prediction claim.

H2 (Subnet-Level Patterns) suggests that subnets prone to being used as pivot sources display distinct structural telemetry compared to non-pivot subnets. Specifically, they are expected to show higher centrality scores and more “bursty” activity patterns. This hypothesis suggests that lateral movement risk is not just a property of individual IP addresses but is also a function of the subnet's position and role within the broader network topology.

H3 (Temporal Consistency) proposes that the structural separation between pivot and non-pivot groups persists when evaluated across different temporal subsets of the dataset (e.g., first 50% chronologically vs. second 50%). This hypothesis tests whether observed patterns are stable over time or artifacts of specific attack campaigns.


### **I.F Methodological Boundaries and Limitations**

To ensure transparency and set appropriate expectations, we explicitly acknowledge critical methodological constraints that bound the scope of this work, namely:

Train/Test Split with Temporal Leakage

The implementation uses a 50/50 temporal train/test split: reconnaissance events from the full dataset of 57,384 windows are sorted chronologically and divided at the median timestamp into 28,692 training samples and 28,692 test samples. The FastRP pivot prototype is computed by averaging embeddings from the training set only (first 50% chronologically), and all reported metrics (AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, Cohen's d, Welch's t-statistic) reflect evaluation on the held-out test set of n = 28,692 (second 50%). This ensures no direct label leakage, i.e. the prototype does not include embeddings from events being evaluated.

However, partial temporal leakage exists in embedding generation in that FastRP embeddings are computed using all edges occurring before the median reconnaissance timestamp (496,818 of 1,898,613 total edges). For the training set (first 50% of events), this ensures proper temporal causality, i.e. the embeddings use only historical data. For the test set (second 50% of events), embeddings incorporate edges from their own detection windows, violating strict per-sample causality. This means test set structural features reflect both historical network context and contemporaneous activity.

The train/test split prevents memorization of specific pivot labels but does not guarantee pure prospective prediction. The reported Cohen's d (0.588) represents a blend of fully-causal predictions (training set evaluated retrospectively) and partially-leaky predictions (test set with contemporaneous structural features). The true causal effect size for prospective prediction is unknown but likely lower.

The implement of a per-sample temporal filtering mechanism where each reconnaissance event at time $t$ uses only edges where “timestamp < t” for embedding generation would potentially ameliorate this current limitation. This would require iterative graph projections (one per evaluation sample).

Dataset Class Imbalance

The UWF-ZeekData24 dataset exhibits a 94.85% pivot rate (27,214 pivots among 28,692 test set reconnaissance windows), inverting typical enterprise network distributions where malicious lateral movement comprises <1% of reconnaissance events. This extreme imbalance has several notable consequences. Reported precision (0.949) is artificially inflated. In operational environments with 1% pivot rates, precision would decrease by 1-2 orders of magnitude even if discrimination capability remained constant. Next, the balanced accuracy of 0.50 indicates performance equivalent to random guessing when classes are equally weighted. Next, model calibration and threshold selection performed on this dataset will not transfer to balanced operational conditions.

The results, however, provide evidence that structural signals exist and can be measured with proper train/test separation, but do not validate operational deployment readiness on realistic class distributions. At current, operational deployment readiness is outside of the scope of this research. However, future work may be done to develop calibration procedures for converting scores from imbalanced training data to realistic operational environments and/or evaluate on balanced datasets (via stratified downsampling or new data collection) and report stratified metrics.

Scope of Claims

Given the above constraints, this work explicitly does not claim validated prospective prediction capability with strict per-sample temporal causality, operational readiness for production SOC deployment without calibration, generalizability to enterprise networks with realistic class distributions (less than 1% pivot rates), or superiority over supervised machine learning approaches.

This work does demonstrate the existence of statistically significant structural differentiation with train/test separation (Cohen's d = 0.588, p < 1e-271, n = 28,692 test samples), proper prototype computation from training data only (no direct label leakage), feasibility of scaling graph analytics to multi-million edge datasets, reproducible infrastructure for community experimentation, and foundational evidence warranting investment in rigorous per-sample temporal filtering and operational validation. These boundaries are maintained consistently throughout the manuscript to ensure scientific integrity and appropriate interpretation of results.

## **Chapter II: Literature Review**

### **II.A MITRE ATT&CK as a Detection Backbone**

The MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) framework is the industry standard for describing adversary tactics and techniques, enabling defenders to map observed network activity to known attack playbooks. The framework was initially introduced in 2013 and has been continuously updated with new techniques (MITRE Corporation, 2023). ATT&CK's taxonomy supports resilient detection by focusing on behavioral patterns rather than easily mutable indicators of compromise. While most research applies ATT&CK reactively to label events post-execution, this work leverages its labels for ground truth in retrospective analysis, enabling statistical characterization of which network positions correlate with labeled lateral movement behaviors.

### **II.B Zeek Telemetry in Threat Hunting**

Zeek (formerly Bro) is a network analysis framework that exports rich connection-level metadata, facilitating high-fidelity threat hunting. Originally developed by Paxson (1999) as the Bro intrusion detection system, Zeek's flow-based approach enables anomaly detection without deep packet inspection, making it effective even in encrypted environments. Ring et al. (2019) demonstrated strong accuracy using Zeek metadata for network anomaly detection. Prior work demonstrates strong accuracy using Zeek metadata alone, and the UWF-ZeekData24 dataset provides a rare combination of real-world APT activity and curated ATT&CK labels, ideal for graph-based structural analysis.

### **II.C Graph-Based Intrusion Detection**

Graph-based approaches to intrusion detection model network interactions as nodes (hosts, IPs) and edges (communications), enabling structural analysis that captures relational context lost in traditional flow-based methods. Early foundational work in anomaly-based intrusion detection by Denning (1987) established statistical models for detecting deviations from normal behavior, while recent advances leverage graph mining techniques to identify suspicious patterns in network topology. Hou et al. (2017) demonstrated the efficacy of system call graphs for malware detection, achieving strong classification performance by modeling process interactions. However, most graph-based intrusion detection systems focus on classification of observed events rather than characterization of structural positions correlating with future adversary actions.

### **II.D  Graph Neural Networks and Embedding Methods**

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) such as GCN (Kipf & Welling, 2017) and GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) learn node representations by aggregating neighborhood information through iterative message passing. These supervised methods excel when labeled training data is abundant but require careful train/test protocols to avoid overfitting. For large graphs and unsupervised settings, random projection methods like FastRP (Neo4j Graph Data Science, 2023) offer scalable alternatives, efficiently generating embeddings that preserve high-order proximity without requiring ground truth labels during embedding generation.

FastRP operates by iteratively projecting the graph's adjacency matrix into lower-dimensional space using sparse random projections, capturing both local neighborhood structure (direct connections) and global community properties (multi-hop paths) with computational efficiency. Bojchevski & Günnemann (2018) demonstrated that random projection-based embeddings can approximate more expensive factorization methods while maintaining representation quality. Qiu et al. (2018) provided theoretical analysis showing that methods like DeepWalk, LINE, and node2vec can be unified under a matrix factorization framework, validating the mathematical foundations of random projection approaches.

Recent advances in Temporal Graph Networks (TGNs) explicitly model time-evolving graph structures with architectures that incorporate edge timestamps as first-class features. Rossi et al. (2020) introduced Temporal Graph Networks (TGN) with memory modules and self-attention mechanisms that aggregate historical interactions, achieving state-of-the-art performance on temporal link prediction tasks. Kumar et al. (2019) developed the Jodie framework for dynamic user-item interaction graphs, demonstrating scalability to millions of temporal events through incremental embedding updates. Other temporal architectures such as TGAT (Xu et al., 2020) and DySAT employ graph attention mechanisms to capture time-varying network structures.

These methods are particularly relevant for network security where temporal ordering is critical for distinguishing attack sequences from benign activity. However, TGNs present practical challenges for the current infrastructure-focused work because they require iterative embedding updates at each timestamp or time window, resulting in O(T) complexity where T is the number of temporal snapshots. For the 1.9M edge dataset spanning multiple days, this would require hundreds of graph projections. Moreover, most TGN architectures (TGAT, DySAT, TGN) require extensive labeled temporal sequences for training, which conflicts with our goal of demonstrating unsupervised baseline characterization. 

FastRP was selected for this work due to its unsupervised nature (no labels required during embedding generation), computational efficiency (single-pass graph projection), and proven scalability to multi-million edge graphs within Neo4j GDS ecosystem (Neo4j, Inc., 2023). Neo4j Labs benchmarks demonstrate FastRP processing graphs with 100M+ edges in minutes on commodity hardware, compared to hours or days for iterative TGN training. This pragmatic choice enables rapid infrastructure prototyping and baseline establishment, with TGN integration left as future work once supervised labels and computational resources are secured. Section 5.4 discusses this trade-off in detail.


### **II.E Lateral Movement Detection in APT Campaigns**

Several studies have addressed lateral movement detection using machine learning and graph analysis. Provenance graph approaches have shown particular promise for APT detection. The MITRE ATT&CK framework (MITRE Corporation, 2023) provides standardized taxonomy for adversarial tactics and techniques, enabling consistent annotation of lateral movement behaviors across diverse datasets. Milajerdi et al. (2019) developed HOLMES, a system for detecting APT campaigns using provenance graphs constructed from system call audit logs. HOLMES employs high-order temporal graph aggregation and information flow tracking to identify suspicious process interactions, demonstrating effectiveness on DARPA engagement datasets. Alsaheel et al. (2021) introduced ATLAS, a system that constructs temporal information flow graphs from system audit logs to reconstruct APT attack campaigns. ATLAS uses graph alignment techniques to match observed activity patterns against known APT behaviors, enabling reconstruction of multi-stage attack sequences on DARPA Transparent Computing datasets. These provenance-based approaches share conceptual similarities with the current work's use of network communication graphs, but operate at system-level (process interactions) rather than network-level (IP communications).

Recent work has applied supervised machine learning to lateral movement detection. For example, ensemble methods such as random forests have demonstrated strong performance on synthetic lateral movement datasets, achieving F1-scores in the 0.85-0.90 range on controlled evaluation data with balanced class distributions and extensive labeled training sets. Han et al. (2020) explored deep learning approaches for detecting lateral movement in enterprise environments, using recurrent neural networks to model temporal sequences in authentication and network access logs. Li et al. (2021) applied graph-based techniques to malicious domain detection, achieving 94% accuracy on DNS query graphs, showing that graph topology encodes attacker behavior patterns. However, these studies operate in supervised classification settings with balanced datasets and access to extensive labeled training data, whereas the current work addresses structural characterization in highly imbalanced real-world APT data using unsupervised embeddings.

Ring et al. (2019) demonstrated 82% accuracy for Zeek-based anomaly detection using flow features, but their approach lacks multi-hop structural context. The current work extends this foundation by incorporating graph-native embeddings that capture n-hop neighborhood patterns, enabling subnet-level analysis and kill chain characterization unavailable to flow-based methods. Unlike supervised approaches that require extensive labeled training data, FastRP embeddings are generated in an unsupervised manner, making them applicable to unlabeled operational environments (though our label-agnostic results in Section 4.3 demonstrate significant challenges with heuristic pivot identification).


### **II.F Scalable Graph Processing Frameworks**

Modern graph analytics rely on efficient dataframe libraries for post-processing large result sets. Polars (Vink, 2023) and Apache Arrow (2023) provide high-performance, memory-efficient data handling through columnar storage, lazy evaluation, and zero-copy sharing. These tools overcome traditional graph database limitations on complex multi-hop pattern matching. NetworkX (Hagberg et al., 2008) provides graph visualization and algorithm implementations in Python, while Neo4j's APOC library (Neo4j, Inc., 2023) enables efficient CSV export and graph manipulation operations.

The integration of Neo4j for graph storage and querying with Polars for heavy analytical computation represents a hybrid architecture that balances the strengths of specialized graph databases (efficient traversal, ACID compliance) with high-performance dataframe processing (streaming joins, memory efficiency). This approach enables the current work to analyze complete multi-hop chain spaces (668M 2-hop sequences) without sampling constraints that limit traditional approaches.


### **II.G Statistical Methods and Evaluation Metrics**

Robust evaluation of analytical frameworks requires appropriate metrics and statistical tests. Precision-Recall curves and ROC curves (Davis & Goadrich, 2006; Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015) assess ranking quality, with AUC-PR particularly informative for imbalanced datasets. Effect size metrics quantify practical significance beyond statistical significance. Cohen's d, introduced by Cohen (1988), measures the standardized difference between two means, with conventional interpretations of 0.2 (small effect), 0.5 (medium effect), and 0.8 (large effect). Welch's t-test (Welch, 1947) provides robust comparison of group means without assuming equal variances, appropriate for comparing pivot and non-pivot embedding distributions.

The machine learning literature provides several techniques for addressing class imbalance, including resampling methods such as SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique; Chawla et al., 2002) and its variants including Borderline-SMOTE (BSMOTE) and SVM-SMOTE, ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling; He et al., 2008), cost-sensitive learning that adjusts misclassification penalties based on class prevalence, and specialized loss functions such as focal loss (Lin et al., 2017) that down-weight easy examples and focus learning on hard minority class cases. Recent empirical work by Bagui et al. (2023) on cybersecurity datasets with rare attack classes found that 10% oversampling using BSMOTE and SVM-SMOTE outperformed higher oversampling ratios (up to 100%), contradicting the conventional practice of achieving 50:50 class balance. This finding is particularly relevant for future work addressing the current dataset's extreme 94.85% pivot rate, suggesting stratified downsampling to 10-30% minority representation may be more effective than full balancing. While these techniques are standard practice in supervised classification, the current work's use of unsupervised FastRP embeddings followed by simple similarity thresholding precludes their direct application. Section 5.4 discusses how future supervised learning approaches could incorporate these methods.

## **Chapter III: Methodology**

### **III.A Experimental Design Overview**

The experimental framework characterizes structural patterns in lateral movement through retrospective analysis of the UWF-ZeekData24 dataset within a Neo4j graph database. The core temporal configuration employs a 48-hour historical window for feature aggregation and embedding generation, followed by a 24-hour detection window for validating pivot behavior. This 48-hour and 24-hour configuration was selected through systematic window optimization experiments that tested multiple temporal splits via the `optimize_windows.py` module, ultimately identifying the configuration positioned on the optimal performance ridgeline of the AUC-ROC surface. This selection balances the competing demands of providing sufficient historical context for stable FastRP embeddings with 128 dimensions; the Neo4j Graph Data Science default represents a balance between expressiveness and computational efficiency and maintaining operational timeliness requirements for detecting pivots before adversary damage escalates. The entire analytical workflow is orchestrated by the `runner.py` module, which manages Docker container lifecycle including startup and teardown, database refresh cycles to ensure clean states between experimental runs, FastRP embedding generation with configurable parameters, and comprehensive analytics export producing synchronized CSV summaries, high-resolution visualizations, and detailed execution logs.

This work implements a pragmatic median-based temporal filtering approach that balances computational feasibility with causal rigor. The system employs a 50/50 temporal train/test split via median timestamp filtering, where the pivot prototype embedding is constructed exclusively from training-set pivots occurring before the median reconnaissance timestamp. This design provides perfect temporal causality for the first 50% of test samples chronologically while enabling infrastructure prototyping at scale through transformation of what would constitute a five-day computational barrier into a three-hour iterative workflow critical for rapid experimentation and baseline establishment during infrastructure prototyping phases. For samples occurring after the median, embeddings incorporate contemporaneous network context from their own detection windows, introducing partial temporal leakage as detailed in Section 1.7.1. This trade-off represents an explicit engineering decision prioritizing feasibility studies and exploratory analysis over rigorous per-sample temporal filtering, which is deferred to operational deployment phases. All samples are evaluated against the training-derived prototype computed exclusively from the first 50% of reconnaissance events chronologically, ensuring no direct label leakage while acknowledging that embedding generation for the second 50% incorporates non-causal network context. The reported metrics in Chapter 4 characterize structural patterns demonstrable within this specific dataset configuration rather than validated prospective prediction capability.


### **III.B Data Preparation**

The data preparation pipeline transforms raw Zeek telemetry from the UWF-ZeekData24 repository into structured graph format suitable for Neo4j ingestion through a multi-stage process. Daily Parquet files containing connection records are loaded into pandas DataFrames, leveraging Parquet's columnar storage format to accelerate reading of large log files compared to row-oriented formats such as CSV or JSON. The filtering and cleaning stage removes duplicate labels to ensure unique edge representation within the graph while filtering records containing null source or destination IP addresses, which comprise less than 0.1% of total rows but would compromise graph integrity as every edge requires valid start and end nodes for traversal algorithms to function correctly.

Feature engineering operations convert raw timestamps to Unix epoch seconds measured from January 1, 1970, enabling numerical temporal arithmetic for duration calculations and temporal filtering operations. The /24 subnet prefix for each IP address is derived using deterministic string parsing that extracts the first three octets, enabling hierarchical analysis at both individual IP address level and subnet aggregate level throughout subsequent analytical stages. During the database loading phase, processed records are written to Neo4j via the official Python driver in batches of 15,000 records to balance transaction overhead against memory consumption. The implementation uses `MERGE` operations rather than `CREATE` statements to ensure idempotency, meaning that pipeline reruns update existing node properties and relationship attributes rather than creating duplicate graph elements that would artificially inflate edge counts and distort centrality calculations.

The UWF-ZeekData24 dataset provides pre-labeled MITRE ATT&CK annotations for each connection record, establishing ground truth for retrospective analysis. Each `CONNECTS` relationship includes three label-related properties that enable both supervised analysis and operational categorization. The `tactic` property captures high-level adversary goals aligned with ATT&CK tactical categories such as Reconnaissance indicating information gathering, Lateral Movement representing adversary navigation between systems, and Credential Access denoting attempts to steal account credentials. The `technique` property specifies the particular method employed using ATT&CK technique identifiers, including T1595 for Active Scanning involving systematic network probing and T1021 for Remote Services representing authenticated pivoting mechanisms. The `is_attack` property provides a binary flag with values of zero or one indicating malicious classification, derived automatically from the presence of non-null `tactic` values enabling efficient filtering of attack-related edges during Cypher queries.

The processed dataset contains 1,898,613 labeled `CONNECTS` relationships representing the attack-focused slice after filtering benign background traffic, distributed across 357 unique IP address nodes that map to 21 distinct /24 subnets reflecting the network's administrative structure. A critical characteristic of this dataset is that all 357 IP addresses participate in at least one attack connection, meaning no nodes exist purely as benign background traffic or isolated hosts—every IP in the network has been either an attacker, a victim, or both within the observed attack campaign. This complete network compromise indicates a highly aggressive adversary with comprehensive reconnaissance coverage and widespread exploitation success, representing an extreme but valuable testbed for analyzing structural patterns in fully-penetrated networks rather than mixed environments containing both compromised and clean segments. Since this data was collected in a simulated, contained environment, these characteristics are sensible. 

Among the 57,384 victim-time pairs classified as reconnaissance windows based on ATT&CK tactic labels, 27,214 windows corresponding to 47.42% of the total confirmed subsequent transition into pivot behavior where the reconnaissance victim later sourced offensive lateral movement activity. This pivot rate of 47.42%, while substantially elevated compared to typical operational enterprise environments where pivots comprise less than 1% of reconnaissance events, creates significant class imbalance that profoundly affects model evaluation, threshold calibration, and generalizability to realistic operational conditions as discussed extensively in Section 5.3.

The Neo4j graph schema implements a labeled property graph model consisting of two primary element types with distinct roles and attribute sets. IP nodes represent unique network endpoints, with each node storing three essential properties: the `address` property containing the full IPv4 address string such as 192.168.1.100 enabling precise endpoint identification, the `subnet` property holding the derived /24 subnet prefix string such as 192.168.1.0/24 supporting hierarchical grouping operations, and the `subnet_id` property providing a numeric identifier for efficient aggregation and filtering in Cypher queries that reference subnet-level patterns. CONNECTS relationships model directed communication between IP nodes, with edges originating at source nodes and terminating at destination nodes to capture the directional nature of network traffic flows. Each relationship stores nine attributes capturing temporal, protocol, and security-relevant metadata: `timestamp` recording connection initiation time in Unix epoch seconds, `duration` measuring connection length in seconds, `service` identifying the application-layer protocol such as HTTP or SSH, `destination_port` specifying the target TCP or UDP port, `connection_state` indicating successful establishment versus failed attempts using Zeek state codes, and the three ATT&CK annotation properties `tactic`, `technique`, and `is_attack` enabling security-focused filtering and analysis operations.
Database performance optimization relies on strategically placed indexes on high-cardinality properties subjected to frequent lookup operations. Indexes on `IP.address` and `IP.subnet` enable sub-second node retrieval during complex graph algorithm execution, critical for FastRP embedding generation that requires iterative neighborhood aggregation across millions of edges. These indexes transform what would otherwise require full node scans with O(n) complexity into indexed lookups with O(log n) complexity, directly impacting the feasibility of running resource-intensive graph data science workflows on commodity hardware within reasonable timeframes.


### **III.C FastRP Embedding Generation**

The core structural analysis component employs the Fast Random Projection algorithm implemented in the Neo4j Graph Data Science library, which generates low-dimensional vector representations for each node by iteratively projecting the graph's adjacency matrix into reduced-dimensional embedding space using sparse random projections. FastRP captures both local neighborhood structure reflecting direct connections and immediate neighbors, and global community properties encoding multi-hop paths and structural equivalence patterns, achieving this dual representation with computational efficiency substantially better than traditional matrix factorization approaches such as singular value decomposition that scale poorly to million-edge graphs.

The implementation configures FastRP with 128 embedding dimensions, representing the Neo4j Graph Data Science default that balances representational expressiveness enabling capture of complex structural patterns against computational efficiency enabling rapid iteration during exploratory analysis. The algorithm executes 4 propagation iterations to capture information from nodes up to four hops away in the graph, deliberately chosen to model typical APT attack chain depths observed in multi-stage campaigns where adversaries rarely extend beyond four or five pivots before reaching target assets or triggering defensive responses. Uniform iteration weights are applied across all four propagation steps rather than employing exponential decay schemes, treating information from one-hop neighbors and four-hop neighbors with equal importance in the final embedding. All embeddings undergo L2-normalization to unit length, transforming embedding vectors such that their Euclidean norm equals one, which enables the use of cosine similarity as the distance metric for prototype comparison while providing scale invariance making embeddings from different graph projections directly comparable. A fixed random seed is configured for the random projection matrices, ensuring reproducibility across pipeline runs and enabling deterministic replication of results for scientific validation purposes.

The temporal filtering implementation follows the median-based approach detailed comprehensively in Section 1.7.1, where embeddings are generated using exclusively those edges occurring before the median reconnaissance timestamp rather than implementing per-sample filtering that would require 28,692 independent graph projections. This approach utilizes 496,818 edges from the full 1,898,613-edge dataset, representing approximately 26% of total edges that occurred in the first half of the temporal observation window. The design ensures perfect temporal causality for the first 50% of test samples chronologically, as their embeddings use only historical edges that occurred strictly before their reconnaissance detection windows. However, for the second 50% of samples, embeddings incorporate network context from edges spanning their own detection windows, introducing partial temporal leakage where contemporaneous structural features influence predictions. This trade-off transforms a 120-hour computational requirement for rigorous per-sample filtering into a three-hour pipeline execution suitable for infrastructure prototyping and rapid baseline establishment. Resulting embeddings are written back to the Neo4j graph as node properties using distinct property names for label-aware and label-agnostic experimental runs to prevent overwriting of results and enable comparative analysis across different methodological approaches.


### **III.D FastRP Embedding Generation and Configuration**

The analytical system implements distinct computational pathways for identifying pivot behavior under two operational paradigms representing different availability of ground truth information. The label-aware mode leverages perfect ground truth provided by curated MITRE ATT&CK labels, enabling retrospective characterization of which structural patterns correlate with confirmed pivot behavior. For each reconnaissance window defined by a victim subnet and temporal detection interval, the system queries Neo4j for all outgoing `CONNECTS` edges originating from IP addresses within the victim subnet during the 24-hour detection window following the reconnaissance event. A reconnaissance window receives positive pivot classification if any cross-subnet edges; those connecting to IP addresses outside the originating /24 subnet boundary; carry MITRE ATT&CK tactic labels associated with offensive post-reconnaissance activity, specifically the tactics of Execution indicating command execution on remote systems, Lateral Movement representing authenticated pivoting, Command and Control denoting adversary-controlled channels, Credential Access reflecting password or token theft, Defense Evasion capturing anti-forensic behaviors, Exfiltration representing data theft, Collection indicating information gathering, or Discovery denoting system reconnaissance. Reconnaissance windows failing to exhibit any such offensive cross-subnet edges during the detection window receive negative non-pivot classification, indicating the reconnaissance activity did not escalate into lateral movement within the observation timeframe.

The label-agnostic mode simulates realistic operational environments where defenders lack perfect attack labels and must rely on traffic-based heuristics to estimate pivot likelihood. Cypher queries retrieve all cross-subnet edges within the detection window regardless of ATT&CK label presence, treating labeled and unlabeled traffic identically to approximate real-world conditions where adversary intentions are unknown at detection time. A Python-implemented heuristic assigns positive pivot classification if a reconnaissance window satisfies either of two volume-based criteria: generation of two or more distinct cross-subnet edges within the 24-hour detection window, or establishment of connections with two or more unique target subnets during the detection interval. This heuristic substantially relaxes the strict label-dependent requirements of label-aware mode, enabling artifact generation and pipeline testing for unlabeled data sources that lack ATT&CK annotations. However, as the results in Section 4.3 demonstrate conclusively, this heuristic approach produces catastrophic performance degradation with negative effect sizes of Cohen's d equals -0.32, indicating that structural embeddings actually anti-correlate with heuristic labels rather than reinforcing them. These label-agnostic results are retained in the manuscript for methodological transparency and to document the fundamental limitations of volume-based heuristics when applied without ground truth validation or per-subnet baseline normalization.

Once pivot status determination completes via either label-aware or label-agnostic pathways, structural similarity computation proceeds through a multi-stage process. Pivot prototype construction creates a 128-dimensional reference embedding by calculating the element-wise mean across FastRP vectors for all reconnaissance windows classified as pivots within the training set, which consists exclusively of the first 50% of samples chronologically ordered. This training-set-only construction ensures no direct label leakage where test sample embeddings influence the prototype against which they are evaluated. Risk score calculation then computes the cosine similarity between each test sample's FastRP embedding and the training-derived pivot prototype, producing continuous scores ranging from negative one indicating opposite structural positions through zero representing orthogonal positions to positive one indicating identical structural signatures. Higher similarity scores indicate greater structural resemblance to confirmed historical pivot patterns, providing a ranking signal for prioritizing reconnaissance alerts during operational triage workflows. For comprehensive comparative evaluation, the system also computes standard structural metrics and temporal activity features known collectively as baseline methods, enabling assessment of whether FastRP embeddings capture information beyond what simpler centrality measures and volume heuristics provide. Section 3.7 provides detailed mathematical definitions for all nine baseline comparison methods employed in the empirical analysis reported in Chapter 4.


### **III.E Structural Similarity Computation and Pivot Classification**

Traditional graph database systems encounter severe memory constraints when materializing extremely large result sets such as complete enumeration of all possible four-hop attack paths through multi-million edge networks. Neo4j's Cypher query language, while optimized for graph traversal and pattern matching, struggles with intermediate result materialization during multi-hop `MATCH` queries that cannot be streamed, frequently exhausting heap memory allocations even when configured with 32 gigabytes of RAM as documented in Section 5.5. To overcome these fundamental architectural limitations, the pipeline implements a hybrid approach that leverages the complementary strengths of graph databases for pattern-based export and dataframe libraries for analytical join operations.

The initial export phase utilizes Neo4j's Awesome Procedures on Cypher library to write all `CONNECTS` relationships with their full attribute sets including source IP address, destination IP address, timestamp in epoch seconds, MITRE ATT&CK tactic and technique labels to flat CSV files stored in shared Docker volumes accessible by both the Neo4j container's internal filesystem and the host machine's Python environment. The lazy loading stage employs Polars' `scan_csv` function to create a `LazyFrame` object representing the CSV data without immediately loading records into RAM. Polars constructs an optimized query execution plan incorporating lazy evaluation strategies such as predicate pushdown that applies filter operations during the CSV scan phase before loading data, projection pruning that reads only required columns rather than entire records, and operation fusion that combines multiple transformation steps into single-pass operations. This lazy evaluation architecture enables streaming processing of datasets substantially larger than available memory by processing data in chunks and writing results progressively to disk rather than materializing complete intermediate results in RAM.

The dynamic hop construction process iterates through chain depths ranging from two hops representing minimal pivot sequences up to ten hops capturing extended multi-stage campaigns. For each target depth n, the algorithm constructs n separate hop frames by aliasing the base edge dataframe and performs incremental self-joins on IP address columns to chain edges together. The three-hop chain construction joining reconnaissance source A through first pivot B through second pivot C to final target D proceeds by joining Hop 1 edges with source A and destination B to Hop 2 edges with source B and destination C, then joining the resulting two-hop intermediate chains to Hop 3 edges with source C and destination D. Each join operation enforces critical temporal constraints requiring that Hop 2 timestamps strictly exceed Hop 1 timestamps and Hop 2 timestamps minus Hop 1 timestamps remain less than 86,400 seconds corresponding to a 24-hour maximum inter-hop interval, maintaining causal ordering and filtering chains with implausibly long dwell times. Uniqueness constraints prevent path loops by filtering chains where any IP address appears multiple times, ensuring each chain represents a distinct sequence of systems rather than circular paths resulting from bidirectional traffic or load balancer configurations. The iterative building strategy constructs two-hop chains first, then extends them to three-hop chains, then four-hop chains, enabling intermediate result reuse where two-hop chains serve as building blocks for longer sequences and progressive search space pruning where chains failing temporal or uniqueness constraints at hop n are excluded before attempting extension to hop n plus one.

The streaming collection mechanism handles query execution by invoking Polars' `collect` method with streaming enabled, which processes the lazy query plan in batches and writes results directly to hop-specific CSV files on disk identified by chain depth such as `chains_2hop.csv` and `chains_4hop.csv`. This streaming approach completely avoids the memory bottleneck of holding entire result sets in RAM simultaneously, enabling the system to materialize hundreds of millions of chains on commodity hardware equipped with only 16 gigabytes of RAM and standard solid-state drives. The final deduplication and aggregation pass reads the generated chain CSV files, removes duplicate chain instances that may emerge from multiple valid traversal orders producing identical IP sequences, and computes comprehensive summary statistics for each hop depth including total chain counts with multiplicity, unique chain counts after deduplication, average number of IP addresses per chain, and timing distributions capturing median, mean, and 95th percentile dwell times between hops.

By strategically partitioning computational responsibilities between Neo4j for initial graph storage and pattern-based edge export and Polars for memory-efficient analytical joins with temporal constraints, the hybrid architecture eliminates the arbitrary sampling constraints that plague pure Cypher approaches limited to tens of thousands of chains by memory ceilings. This design choice enables comprehensive analysis of complete attack propagation patterns across the full spectrum of chain lengths without the systematic bias toward shorter chains introduced by sampling strategies that preferentially retain chains fitting in available memory while discarding longer chains representing sustained multi-stage campaigns.


### **III.F Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Validation**
Performance assessment employs a comprehensive suite of standard classification metrics and statistical hypothesis tests selected to address the specific challenges posed by extreme class imbalance and the need for rigorous significance testing across multiple comparison methods. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve, abbreviated AUC-ROC, measures discrimination ability across all possible decision thresholds by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate, with values ranging from 0.5 indicating performance equivalent to random guessing to 1.0 representing perfect classification capability. For this work, AUC-ROC quantifies how effectively structural similarity scores rank actual pivots above non-pivots across the full spectrum of threshold values, providing a threshold-independent assessment of ranking quality. The Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve, abbreviated AUC-PR, summarizes the precision-recall trade-off across all thresholds by plotting precision against recall, providing a more reliable performance indicator than AUC-ROC for highly imbalanced datasets where the negative class dramatically outnumbers the positive class in operational environments. Given this dataset's 94.85% pivot rate that inverts typical operational distributions, AUC-PR directly measures the balance between false positives and false negatives without the optimistic bias that affects AUC-ROC when class distributions are skewed.

Welch's t-test provides statistical validation by comparing means of two independent groups, specifically the pivot and non-pivot embedding similarity distributions, without assuming equal population variances that may not hold when one class exhibits substantially higher variance than the other. The test evaluates the null hypothesis that both groups have identical mean similarity to the prototype, with rejection of this null hypothesis providing evidence for structural differentiation. All reported p-values employ two-tailed tests that assess whether the observed difference in either direction; pivots scoring higher or lower than non-pivots; is statistically significant, avoiding directional assumptions that could bias interpretation. Cohen's d effect size metric quantifies the magnitude of difference between two means expressed in pooled standard deviation units, enabling interpretation of whether statistically significant differences represent practically meaningful separation in embedding space. The conventional interpretation thresholds classify effect sizes as small at 0.2 standard deviations, medium at 0.5 standard deviations, and large at 0.8 standard deviations. This metric proves essential for determining whether the observed structural differentiation, while achieving statistical significance through large sample sizes, translates to discriminative power sufficient for operational deployment rather than merely reflecting statistically detectable but practically negligible differences.

Multiple comparison correction addresses the inflation of family-wise error rate when conducting numerous simultaneous hypothesis tests across the nine baseline methods evaluated in Section 4.2. Without correction, testing nine independent hypotheses at the conventional alpha equals 0.05 significance level would yield an approximately 37% probability of observing at least one false positive purely by chance even when no true effects exist. The Bonferroni correction method controls this family-wise error rate by dividing the desired alpha level by the number of comparisons, adjusting the significance threshold to alpha equals 0.05 divided by 9 yielding approximately 0.0056. This conservative approach ensures that all reported statistically significant findings survive the correction, maintaining the overall false positive rate at 5% across the complete family of tests rather than per individual test. Balanced accuracy computes the arithmetic mean of sensitivity, defined as the true positive rate or recall, and specificity, defined as the true negative rate, providing a class-balanced performance metric that treats both classes with equal importance regardless of their frequencies in the dataset. This metric proves critical for assessing performance on imbalanced datasets where raw accuracy can be misleading, as a naive classifier predicting the majority class for all samples would achieve 94.85% accuracy on this dataset despite providing zero discriminative value.

The baseline method comparison suite includes six features representing established structural metrics and temporal activity patterns. Maximum betweenness centrality identifies the highest betweenness centrality score among all nodes within the reconnaissance window's subnet during the historical period, where betweenness centrality measures how frequently a node lies on shortest paths between other node pairs in the graph, effectively capturing bridge nodes that connect distinct network regions and control information flow. Average PageRank computes the mean PageRank score across subnet nodes during the historical window, quantifying node importance based on the weighted combination of incoming edge counts and the importance scores of source nodes, implementing the foundational algorithm underlying web search ranking. Average clustering coefficient calculates the mean local clustering coefficient for subnet nodes, measuring the proportion of a node's neighbors that are also connected to each other, effectively quantifying local triangle density and community cohesion. Subnet size provides a simple count of unique IP addresses observed within the /24 subnet during the historical window, capturing subnet scale as a potential confounding variable where larger subnets may exhibit different pivot patterns than smaller isolated segments.

Burst score quantifies the temporal burstiness of network activity by computing the standard deviation of hourly connection counts divided by the mean hourly count, where high values indicate intermittent intense activity bursts characteristic of automated scanning or batch credential theft, while low values indicate steady background traffic typical of routine services. Connection velocity measures the rate of new unique destination IP contacts per hour during the historical window, capturing reconnaissance activity intensity where rapidly expanding connection sets may indicate active scanning compared to static communication patterns of legitimate services. All features are computed using exclusively historical data occurring before the reconnaissance window start timestamp to maintain temporal causality for the training set, ensuring that feature values cannot incorporate information from future events that would constitute temporal leakage. Features undergo min-max normalization to the range zero to one for fair comparison across methods with different natural scales, preventing features with large numeric ranges from dominating distance calculations. Similarity scores for FastRP and raw normalized feature values for baseline methods are used to rank reconnaissance windows from highest to lowest risk, with performance evaluated via AUC-ROC measuring ranking quality, AUC-PR assessing precision-recall balance, and Cohen's d effect size quantifying the magnitude of separation between pivot and non-pivot distributions.

All results reported throughout Chapter 4 stem from the analysis run executed on November 20, 2025, utilizing the median-based temporal filtering approach described in Section 3.4 with 496,818 edges occurring before the median reconnaissance timestamp at Unix epoch second 1,677,650,400 used for FastRP embedding generation across all 57,384 reconnaissance windows (split 50/50 into 28,692 training samples for prototype construction and 28,692 test samples for evaluation).

## **Chapter IV: Results and Analysis**
Before evaluating structural similarity metrics, exploratory analysis of graph structure and temporal dynamics was conducted to understand underlying patterns of adversary behavior and establish empirical context for subsequent statistical characterization. This preliminary analysis revealed three critical patterns spanning pivot concentration, temporal dynamics, and tactic distribution that fundamentally shaped the analytical approach and interpretation of results.

Analysis of pivot source distribution reveals extreme concentration of offensive activity at both individual host and subnet levels. Although the dataset contains 357 unique IP addresses distributed across 21 subnets, the 877,795 cross-subnet lateral movement attacks originate from merely 14 specific IP addresses representing 3.9% of the network, averaging 62,700 attacks per pivoting IP. Attack concentration follows a heavy-tailed distribution where the top 5 IPs (1.4% of network, 35.7% of pivoting nodes) account for 50% of all lateral movement, the top 8 IPs contribute 75%, and the most active 13 IPs represent 99% of attack volume. Activity level analysis reveals that 13 of the 14 pivoting IPs (92.9%) fall into the "Very High" category (>10,000 attacks each), with only a single IP exhibiting medium activity (407 attacks), indicating that adversaries establish strong footholds on carefully selected bridge nodes and exploit them repeatedly for subsequent attacks rather than distributing lateral movement attempts uniformly across compromised infrastructure.

Critically, all 14 lateral movement sources are classified as pivots in graph-theoretic terms; nodes that were both victims of incoming attacks and subsequently launched their own attacks; with zero lateral movement originating from initial attacker nodes that were never compromised. This finding demonstrates that adversaries in this campaign exclusively leverage compromised infrastructure for lateral movement propagation rather than directly attacking from external command-and-control nodes, aligning with established APT tradecraft emphasizing "living off the land" by using victim resources to avoid detection. This concentration pattern intensifies at subnet level across all 21 network segments, where 14 subnets each contribute exactly one pivoting IP, ranging from 143.88.3.0/24 sourcing 125,338 attacks (14.3% of total) down to 143.88.11.0/24 contributing only 407 attacks (0.05%). In sharp contrast, subnet `143.88.10.0/24` exhibits completely inverted behavior, serving as a heavily targeted reconnaissance victim with 1,181 distinct detection windows yet never once transitioning into a pivot source during the entire observation period. This dormant profile, where substantial incoming reconnaissance activity fails to precipitate any offensive lateral movement, validates the fundamental need for analytical frameworks capable of distinguishing between high-volume scanning targets that remain passive victims and actual compromised staging points that adversaries weaponize for campaign expansion.

DNS servers processing thousands of name resolution requests daily maintain connections to nearly every subnet for recursive query resolution. Domain controllers handle authentication traffic across all network segments through centralized Active Directory services. NTP servers synchronize time across diverse subnets through periodic polling mechanisms. Authorized vulnerability scanners conduct security assessments by systematically probing systems across multiple segments. Asset management platforms inventory network resources through automated discovery across organizational boundaries. This heuristic conflation creates a grossly heterogeneous pivot group representing a noisy statistical average of genuine attackers exhibiting targeted structural exploitation patterns optimized for stealthy multi-hop progression and benign heavy users exhibiting omnidirectional high-volume communication patterns serving legitimate administrative functions.
The resulting dilution transforms the heuristic pivot prototype from a coherent structural signature characterizing adversarial bridge exploitation into an incoherent amalgamation of incompatible topological patterns. True APT pivots—as demonstrated by the 14 confirmed source IPs averaging 62,700 attacks each with extreme concentration where the top 5 account for 50% of lateral movement—exhibit selective high-volume targeting of specific downstream victims enabling campaign expansion. Benign infrastructure exhibits indiscriminate high-volume connectivity across broad swaths of the network serving operational functions. These fundamentally incompatible structural patterns, when averaged together through heuristic mislabeling, produce a prototype that poorly represents either category.
Conversely, the minuscule set of 3,118 heuristic non-pivots representing only 0.26% of windows consists of systems so profoundly isolated or inactive that they failed to generate even two cross-subnet edges during their 24-hour detection windows. These extremely quiet systems—typically standalone workstations with minimal inter-subnet communication, air-gapped development systems intentionally isolated from production networks, or dormant infrastructure awaiting decommissioning—exhibit minimal cross-subnet connectivity that paradoxically resembles the structural prototype of true stealthy pivots more closely than the heuristically labeled pivot group dominated by chatty infrastructure. True adversarial pivots, despite launching substantial attack volumes when viewed as aggregate campaigns over extended periods, often exhibit relatively modest connection counts within individual 24-hour windows as adversaries deliberately throttle activity to evade rate-based detection. The heuristic non-pivot group, by filtering to only the most isolated systems, inadvertently captures structural patterns closer to this stealthy profile than the high-volume administrative systems dominating the heuristic pivot group, producing the negative correlation where heuristic non-pivots score higher similarity to the true pivot prototype than heuristic pivots.
Comparative evaluation across baseline methods reveals differential robustness to label noise, with temporal features maintaining discriminative capability while structural features uniformly fail under heuristic conditions. Connection Velocity, measuring the rate of new unique destination IP contacts per hour, achieves AUC-ROC of 0.717 with positive Cohen's d of 1.04, indicating that temporal activity patterns successfully differentiate heuristic pivot and non-pivot groups even when ground truth labels are severely corrupted by inclusion of benign traffic. Subnet Size similarly maintains positive discrimination with Cohen's d of 0.89. In sharp contrast, all structural graph-based features exhibit negative correlations, with FastRP at Cohen's d of -0.32, Burst Score at -0.67, Maximum PageRank at -0.97, and Average Clustering Coefficient at -1.00. All reported statistical tests achieve significance well below the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of p less than 0.0056, confirming that these negative correlations represent systematic anti-correlation rather than random noise.
This stark divergence between temporal and structural feature robustness suggests that temporal activity patterns capture adversary behavior characteristics that remain detectable even when labels are contaminated by benign high-volume traffic, while topological properties prove brittle to the structural heterogeneity introduced by heuristic mislabeling. Administrative systems exhibit temporal burstiness and connection velocity profiles that differ sufficiently from both true pivots and isolated workstations to enable partial discrimination. However, their structural positions as legitimate bridge nodes connecting multiple subnets for operational purposes create topological signatures that systematically confound graph-based embeddings trained on true adversarial pivot patterns optimized for stealthy campaign expansion rather than routine service delivery.
These findings demonstrate the fundamental insufficiency of volume-based heuristics for pivot identification without ground truth calibration or per-subnet baseline normalization accounting for legitimate administrative traffic patterns. The results validate the analytical framework's ability to expose heuristic failure modes through rigorous statistical evaluation, but simultaneously underscore that structural embeddings alone provide inadequate information in label-agnostic operational settings. Future deployment in environments lacking ground truth labels would require fundamental redesign incorporating temporal features as primary signals complemented by structural features, semi-supervised learning leveraging small sets of confirmed pivot labels to calibrate heuristics, per-subnet baseline traffic modeling to identify anomalous deviations from expected administrative patterns rather than applying uniform absolute thresholds, or ensemble approaches combining multiple signal types with dynamic weighting based on observed label quality. Given the magnitude of performance degradation under label-agnostic conditions—negative effect sizes indicating anti-correlation rather than mere absence of signal—operational deployment claims are restricted to scenarios with validated ground truth labels until these fundamental methodological challenges are addressed through future research.

The dataset contains 667,649,904 distinct 2-hop chains representing minimal reconnaissance-to-pivot-to-target sequences, where initial reconnaissance of a victim subnet leads to first pivot launching attack against final target. These 2-hop patterns, encompassing approximately 250 million unique IP sequences averaging three nodes per chain, represent the foundational building blocks of lateral movement where adversaries exploit initial footholds to reach immediate secondary targets.

As campaign depth increases, chain frequency declines exponentially through systematic application of temporal causality constraints and uniqueness filters. This rapid initial pivot reflects automated exploitation pipelines, widespread credential reuse enabling immediate authenticated access, or exploitation of unpatched vulnerabilities allowing swift compromise without requiring extended reconnaissance or custom exploit development.
However, the subsequent progression from first pivot to second pivot extends to median dwell time of 40.65 hours, representing a dramatic 99-fold increase in delay compared to the initial reconnaissance-to-pivot interval. This profound slowdown at the second hop indicates that while initial network breach proceeds rapidly through opportunistic exploitation of exposed vulnerabilities, subsequent campaign expansion becomes substantially more calculated as adversaries shift from automated penetration to deliberate navigation requiring human operator involvement. The third-to-fourth pivot transition extends to median 89.34 hours and mean 138.92 hours, nearly doubling the initial reconnaissance-to-pivot interval and indicating that adversaries allocate multiple days between successive pivots when operating deep within defended infrastructure. The characteristic pattern of rapid initial compromise followed by progressively slower expansion strongly suggests that adversaries adopt increasingly cautious operational tempo as they penetrate deeper into defended infrastructure, allocating extended periods to thoroughly explore newly compromised hosts through systematic directory enumeration and file system inspection, harvest stored credentials and authentication tokens from memory dumps and cached files, meticulously map potential high-value targets accessible from current positions through network scanning and Active Directory queries, and carefully plan subsequent moves to minimize defensive detection risk by selecting targets, timing attacks during low-monitoring periods, and employing anti-forensic techniques.
The slow burn behavior where initial breach proceeds rapidly but subsequent expansion unfolds over days or weeks provides critical operational windows for defensive containment. Detection and response systems achieving sub-24-hour response latency during initial reconnaissance-to-pivot phases may successfully isolate compromised infrastructure before adversaries establish secondary footholds at the 40-hour median second-pivot interval. This temporal cushion suggests that even moderately delayed defensive responses—operating on timescales of one to two days rather than real-time alerting—retain meaningful containment value for preventing campaign propagation beyond initial compromise, provided that detection mechanisms reliably identify first-pivot activity before adversaries complete their deliberate planning and preparation for second-pivot execution. The progressive lengthening of dwell times at deeper stages reinforces this finding, indicating that each successive hop provides defenders with increasingly extended windows for detection and remediation before adversaries execute next moves, though the extreme patience exhibited by some campaigns at 95th percentile demonstrates that determined adversaries willing to maintain persistent access for weeks or months may eventually evade even delayed defensive responses.

## **Chapter V: Discussion**

The results provide a nuanced view of capabilities and limitations of graph-based structural characterization for lateral movement analysis. The label-aware experiments offer strong support for the core premise: structural embeddings encode meaningful information correlating with lateral movement behavior. The Cohen's d effect size of 0.588 is a significant finding which indicates that even without knowing specific traffic content (payloads), the mere shape of a node's interactions (who it talks to, how often, in what pattern) provides a measurable signal about its role. This validates FastRP as a feature extraction method for security graphs. However, the moderate AUC-ROC (0.618) and balanced accuracy (0.50) suggest structure is necessary but insufficient. It captures the potential of a node's structural position but perhaps not the kinetic trigger of actual exploitation. The observation that temporal features like Burst Score (d = 0.877) achieve stronger discrimination indicates that combining structural embeddings with granular temporal signals (inter-arrival time variance, burst patterns) or behavioral markers (specific error codes, authentication patterns) would likely be required for operational systems.
The discovery that FastRP embeddings exhibit only 14 unique values across 28,692 samples (clustering at /24 boundaries) has important implications for operational deployment. This subnet-level clustering reduces overfitting risk through fewer degrees of freedom, aligns naturally with operational triage workflows where analysts think in terms of network segments, and provides interpretable groupings that match administrative boundaries. However, this granularity limits fine-grained discrimination between individual hosts within the same subnet and may miss within-subnet heterogeneity where different host types (workstations versus servers) coexist in the same /24 block. These characteristics suggest the framework is most appropriate for subnet-level risk assessment rather than individual host classification, matching the operational reality that containment and investigation often occur at network segment boundaries.
The negative effect size (-0.32) in label-agnostic mode tells a cautionary tale about the limitations of volume-based heuristics. When broad heuristics define pivots, the signal is diluted and the pivot prototype becomes a noisy average of actual attackers and benign heavy users. The fact that heuristic non-pivots looked more like the true labeled prototype than heuristic pivots did implies the heuristic captured a broad set of busy behaviors structurally distinct from stealthy, targeted APT movements. High-volume infrastructure nodes such as DNS servers, domain controllers, and NTP servers generate substantial cross-subnet traffic that triggers simple volume thresholds while exhibiting fundamentally different structural patterns than adversarial lateral movement. This finding highlights the danger of relying solely on volume-based heuristics in high-noise environments and underscores the necessity of ground truth labels or complementary temporal features for effective pivot identification.


### **V.A Operational Implications**

While this work demonstrates retrospective characterization rather than validated prospective prediction, the findings suggest several conceptual directions for operational security workflows that warrant rigorous validation. Structural risk scores derived from FastRP embeddings could complement existing reconnaissance alerts as ranking signals rather than standalone triggers. When analysts encounter reconnaissance alerts, associated structural similarity scores would provide additional context about whether victim nodes occupy network positions historically associated with pivot behavior, helping prioritize investigation queues when multiple alerts compete for attention. However, operational deployment would require substantial recalibration for realistic pivot rates (typically below 1%, compared to this dataset's 94.85%) and validation on representative operational data before threshold recommendations could be made with confidence.
The subnet-level granularity exhibited by embeddings (14 unique values across 21 subnets) aligns naturally with Security Operations Center containment workflows, where investigations and isolation measures occur at network segment boundaries rather than individual host level. The observed subnet concentration; where three specific /24 blocks sourced the vast majority of pivots; suggests that subnet-level risk assessment may be appropriate for network topologies with clear administrative boundaries. This finding supports the hypothesis that adversaries exploit structural properties of network segments, such as bridge subnets connecting multiple VLANs, rather than selecting arbitrary individual hosts. Containment strategies leveraging these insights would prioritize isolating high-risk segments when pivots are confirmed, though the specificity of these patterns to this dataset and network architecture requires validation across diverse operational environments.
The multi-hop timing analysis provides empirical evidence for defender response timeframes. The median 40-hour interval between first and second pivots suggests a potential operational window for containment before adversary footprint expands significantly. This temporal pattern; rapid initial compromise (median 0.41 hours from reconnaissance to first pivot) followed by deliberate expansion; indicates that detection and response playbooks should prioritize speed during initial stages while maintaining sustained monitoring over subsequent days. However, these timing windows reflect adversary behaviors specific to this APT campaign. Different network environments, adversary profiles, and defensive capabilities would likely exhibit different temporal dynamics, making generalization without empirical validation inappropriate.
Operational deployment of structural risk scoring would require continuous model calibration through feedback loops tracking precision at top-K rankings, false positive rates across alert tiers, and time-to-containment metrics. The extreme class imbalance observed (94.85% pivots) means that threshold values and performance estimates would not transfer directly to operational environments with realistic pivot rates. Precision estimates from this work are likely optimistic by one to two orders of magnitude for production deployment. The balanced accuracy of 0.50 indicates that classification performance on balanced operational data would require substantial improvement through ensemble methods combining structural embeddings with temporal and behavioral features. These calibration requirements underscore that the current work provides proof-of-concept evidence for structural signal existence rather than a deployment-ready operational system.


### **V.B Methodological Limitations and Constraints**

The experimental design incorporates several methodological constraints that fundamentally bound the scope and interpretation of results. Understanding these limitations is essential for contextualizing findings and establishing appropriate expectations for future work building upon this foundation.
The most critical limitation is the absence of separate held-out test set reporting. While the experimental design implements train/test splitting via median-based temporal filtering; where the pivot prototype is computed exclusively from training-set pivots (first 50% chronologically) and all samples are evaluated against this training-derived prototype; the reported metrics aggregate performance across both training and test sets rather than reporting held-out test performance separately. This means the reported statistics (p < 1e-271, AUC-ROC of 0.618, Cohen's d of 0.588) represent overall dataset characterization rather than validated predictive performance on unseen data. While this approach successfully prevents direct label leakage and maintains evaluation integrity, it cannot support claims about generalization to new reconnaissance windows outside this dataset. The reported performance metrics describe how well structural patterns correlate with pivot behavior in this specific dataset configuration rather than validated prospective prediction capability. Future work must report performance metrics separately for training and held-out test sets (ideally with additional validation set for hyperparameter tuning), enabling assessment of generalization capability and model stability. While test-set-only metrics are expected to show similar effect sizes given the train/test protocol already implemented, separate reporting would provide scientifically rigorous evidence for generalization claims.
The median-based temporal filtering approach represents a deliberate engineering trade-off between computational feasibility and causal rigor. Rather than implementing per-sample temporal filtering where each reconnaissance event uses only edges occurring strictly before its timestamp, this work filters the entire edge set to those occurring before the median reconnaissance timestamp. This design provides partial causality guarantees which is the first 50% of samples (chronologically) maintain perfect temporal causality with embeddings computed from truly historical edges, while the second 50% incorporate contemporaneous network context from edges spanning their detection windows. The computational motivation for this choice is substantial: median filtering enables 3-hour pipeline execution, whereas rigorous per-sample filtering would require approximately 120 hours on commodity hardware due to the need for 28,692 independent graph projections. 
Beyond computational efficiency, the median cutoff ensures sufficient edge density (496,818 of 1.9M edges) for stable FastRP embeddings, avoiding the sparse graph artifacts that emerge when early-timestamp samples have minimal historical context. The reported Cohen's d of 0.588 represents a weighted average of fully causal predictions (first 50%, estimated d≈0.45-0.50) and contemporaneous-context predictions (second 50%, estimated d ≈ 0.65-0.70), with all samples evaluated against a prototype computed exclusively from training-set pivots to maintain evaluation integrity. This filtering approach successfully removes inflated performance from future information leakage, as demonstrated by the label-agnostic AUC-ROC decreasing from 0.422 (full graph) to 0.293 (median-filtered). The median-based design is appropriate for infrastructure feasibility studies and baseline establishment, enabling rapid iteration while maintaining partial causal guarantees. Future operational deployment would require transitioning to strict per-sample temporal filtering through incremental graph updates via Neo4j streaming API, daily embedding pre-computation with interpolation for individual samples, or parallel projection generation across compute clusters, with an expected computational investment of one to two weeks for this dataset scale and performance metrics showing Cohen's d likely in the 0.45-0.55 range.
The extreme dataset class imbalance represents a critical limitation that profoundly affects result interpretation. The UWF-ZeekData24 dataset exhibits a 94.85% pivot rate, completely inverting typical enterprise distributions where pivots comprise less than 1% of reconnaissance events. This imbalance manifests in several artificial performance characteristics: perfect recall of 1.000 occurs simply because predicting "pivot" for every sample succeeds 95% of the time given the base rate, making recall an uninformative metric. The reported precision of 0.949 is artificially inflated and would be approximately 10-100 times lower in operational environments with realistic 1% pivot rates, even if the underlying discrimination capability remained identical. The balanced accuracy of 0.50 reveals that performance is equivalent to random guessing when classes are weighted equally, indicating that apparent success stems entirely from exploiting class distribution rather than genuine pattern recognition. Furthermore, the attack-focused curation means the dataset emphasizes APT activity while filtering out the vast majority of benign traffic that would exist in operational monitoring environments, creating a fundamentally unrepresentative evaluation context. 
The combined impact of these factors is that results demonstrate structural signals can be detected when pivots are common, but provide no evidence these signals would remain detectable at realistic operational noise levels. Model calibration and threshold selection developed on this dataset will not transfer to operational environments, and precision estimates are optimistic by one to two orders of magnitude for production deployment. These findings provide valuable proof-of-concept evidence that structural differentiation is measurable in principle, but they cannot support operational deployment claims without validation on balanced datasets representative of real-world conditions. Future work must evaluate performance on stratified downsampled datasets, collect or synthesize realistic operational data with pivot rates below 5%, and develop calibration procedures for converting scores from imbalanced training distributions to operational contexts.
The absence of supervised learning baselines represents a significant gap in the experimental design. This work compares FastRP unsupervised embeddings against basic centrality metrics and temporal heuristics, but never establishes comparison against state-of-the-art supervised graph neural network methods such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), Graph Attention Networks (GAT), or GraphSAGE trained with ground truth labels. Given that the dataset includes perfect MITRE ATT&CK labels for all connections, supervised classifiers leveraging these labels during training would be expected to achieve substantially higher discrimination, likely exceeding 0.85 AUC-ROC. Without these baselines, readers cannot determine whether FastRP's moderate performance of 0.618 AUC-ROC represents fundamental limits of structural signals or merely reflects the inherent limitations of unsupervised methods that do not leverage label information during model construction. This ambiguity is scientifically significant: if supervised methods dramatically outperform FastRP (the expected outcome), it would suggest that structural signals contain substantially more information than unsupervised embeddings capture, warranting reframing of this work as an "unsupervised approximation for label-scarce environments" rather than a definitive assessment of structural signal strength. Conversely, if supervised and unsupervised methods perform comparably, it would be a surprising and theoretically interesting finding suggesting that random projection captures most available structural information. Future work must train at least one supervised GNN baseline with proper train/test splits, report comparative results alongside existing baselines, and discuss the computational and labeling cost trade-offs where FastRP requires no training time or labeled data while supervised GNNs require extensive training with labeled examples.
The label-agnostic mode demonstrates fundamental limitations of volume-based heuristics for pivot identification. The heuristic approach; classifying windows as pivots if they exhibit two or more cross-subnet edges or contact two or more unique target subnets; produces a negative effect size (Cohen's d = -0.32), indicating that structural embeddings actually anti-correlate with heuristic labels. This counterintuitive finding reflects three underlying causes: the heuristic captures benign high-volume infrastructure (DNS servers, NTP servers, domain controllers) as frequently as true attack pivots since these systems naturally generate substantial cross-subnet traffic in normal operations; the volume-based threshold lacks baseline normalization, treating two edges identically across all subnets despite dramatic differences in typical activity levels where two edges may represent routine behavior for high-traffic subnets but extreme anomaly for isolated segments; and the heuristic ignores all temporal context, failing to distinguish between two edges occurring over 24 hours (likely routine) versus two edges within one minute (potentially indicative of scanning). The practical consequence is that label-agnostic mode cannot be operationally deployed without fundamental redesign, as heuristic-generated labels prove unsuitable for training or validating structural models and serve primarily as a documented negative result demonstrating these limitations. The scientific lesson is that structural embeddings alone provide insufficient information in label-agnostic settings, while temporal features such as Connection Velocity (d = 1.04) maintain positive discrimination even with noisy heuristic labels, strongly suggesting that ensemble approaches combining structural and temporal signals are required. Future work should investigate semi-supervised or weak supervision approaches leveraging small sets of confirmed pivots, test ensemble models that integrate FastRP embeddings with temporal features, and develop per-subnet baseline traffic models to identify anomalous deviations rather than applying uniform absolute thresholds.
Finally, generalizability constraints limit the scope of conclusions that can be drawn from these results. Findings are specific to the particular characteristics of the UWF-ZeekData24 dataset; 357 IP addresses across 21 subnets observed over approximately 30 days; and to the particular APT campaign captured in this data, which reflects specific adversary tactics, techniques, and preferences as well as a specific network topology and administrative structure. The decision to aggregate at /24 subnet boundaries may not generalize to networks with different address allocation schemes, such as large enterprises using /16 or /8 allocations where subnet-level patterns may not emerge as clearly. The small network size of 357 IP addresses limits assessment of how methods would scale to enterprise networks with thousands or tens of thousands of hosts, where computational requirements and signal-to-noise ratios may differ substantially. The 30-day temporal span may miss seasonal patterns, long-term campaign evolution, or periodic behaviors that operate on monthly or quarterly cycles. These constraints mean that claims about method transferability to different network architectures (cloud environments, IoT networks, operational technology and industrial control systems), scalability to larger networks, or temporal stability over extended periods cannot be supported without additional validation. Future work must validate findings on multiple datasets from different organizations and network topologies, evaluate scalability systematically on networks with 1K, 10K, and 100K nodes, and test temporal stability across longer observation periods spanning three months, six months, and one year to establish the boundaries of applicability with empirical evidence.


### **V.C Lessons Learned and Design Trade-Offs**

Infrastructure development revealed several critical design decisions, computational trade-offs, and failed approaches that warrant documentation to guide future work and prevent replicated effort. These lessons span temporal filtering strategies, embedding method selection, heuristic design failures, granularity implications, and data processing architecture choices.
The median-based temporal filtering approach represents a strategic decision with significant implications for both computational feasibility and causal validity. Rather than implementing per-sample filtering where each reconnaissance event uses only edges occurring strictly before its timestamp, this work filters the entire edge set to those occurring before the median reconnaissance timestamp, using 496,818 of the full 1.9 million edges for embedding generation. This design enables feasibility studies at scale by transforming what would be a 5-day computational barrier into a 3-hour iterative workflow, critical for rapid experimentation and baseline establishment during infrastructure prototyping. The approach preserves perfect temporal causality for the first 50% of samples chronologically, providing clean validation data for initial hypothesis testing without any temporal leakage contamination. Additionally, the median cutoff maintains sufficient edge density for stable FastRP embeddings, avoiding the sparse graph pathologies that plague per-event filtering at early timestamps when minimal historical context exists. The single projection shared across all samples enables deterministic results and simplified debugging compared to managing 28,692 unique projections with varying edge sets.
The computational reality underlying this decision is substantial. Per-sample filtering requires O(n) graph projections where n equals 28,692 test samples, with each projection involving a Cypher query to export temporally-filtered edges taking 8-12 seconds, graph projection creation consuming 3-5 seconds, FastRP embedding computation requiring 2-4 seconds, and similarity calculation completing in under one second. The estimated runtime of 15-20 seconds per sample multiplied by 28,692 samples yields over 120 hours on commodity hardware including M1 Pro processor with 32 gigabytes of RAM. This 40-fold computational overhead would have blocked infrastructure development and exploratory analysis within project timeline constraints, making iterative refinement practically infeasible. The trade-off explicitly accepted is that contemporaneous network context for the second 50% of samples provides upper-bound performance estimates rather than pure prospective prediction, appropriate for infrastructure feasibility studies and baseline establishment while deferring rigorous per-sample filtering to operational deployment phases. Future work should implement per-sample filtering using incremental graph updates rather than full re-projections through the Neo4j streaming API, pre-compute embeddings at daily granularity then interpolate for individual samples to amortize computational cost, and consider approximate methods such as sampling 10% of samples for per-sample evaluation and extrapolating results to the full dataset, budgeting 1-2 weeks of computational time for rigorous temporal filtering on this dataset scale.
The selection of FastRP unsupervised embeddings over Temporal Graph Networks including TGAT, TGN, and DySAT architectures reflects both pragmatic and methodological considerations. FastRP's unsupervised nature allows embedding generation without requiring labels during training, enabling application to both labeled retrospective analysis and unlabeled prospective deployment settings, whereas Temporal Graph Networks require extensive labeled temporal sequences for supervised training. Implementation maturity strongly favors FastRP, as Neo4j Graph Data Science library provides production-grade FastRP with optimized Cypher integration, monitoring capabilities, and memory management, while TGN frameworks such as PyTorch Geometric Temporal and Deep Graph Library require custom database connectors and lack battle-tested scalability at the multi-million edge scale. The computational profiles differ dramatically: FastRP requires a single graph projection consuming 3-5 minutes for 1.9 million edges, whereas TGNs require iterative training across temporal snapshots estimated at 2-4 hours for this dataset plus extensive hyperparameter tuning. For baseline establishment, it is pragmatic to first establish whether any graph-based signal exists before investing in complex temporal architectures, and FastRP provides this baseline efficiently. The trade-off accepted is loss of explicit temporal dynamics where TGNs model edge timestamp evolution in exchange for rapid infrastructure prototyping and unsupervised capability. Future work should consider TGN integration if supervised baselines show AUC-ROC exceeding 0.75, indicating sufficient signal for complex models to extract, employ a hybrid approach where FastRP provides initial filtering of the top 20% risk scores with TGN applied for detailed analysis, and benchmark TGN runtime on smaller network subsets of approximately 100 IP addresses before committing to full-scale deployment.
The failure of label-agnostic volume heuristics provides important negative results demonstrating fundamental limitations of unsupervised pivot identification. The heuristic approach classifying windows as pivots if they exhibit two or more cross-subnet edges or contact two or more unique target subnets produces a negative effect size of Cohen's d equals -0.32, indicating that structural embeddings actually anti-correlate with heuristic labels rather than reinforcing them. Root cause analysis reveals three critical failures. First, the heuristic is baseline blind, applying uniform thresholds across all subnets where high-volume subnets such as DNS and Active Directory controllers generate over 50 daily cross-subnet edges normally, making a 2-edge threshold meaningless, while low-volume subnets may see 0-1 edges per week making 2 edges highly anomalous. Second, the heuristic exhibits temporal ignorance, making no distinction between 2 edges occurring in 1 minute potentially indicative of scanning versus 2 edges spread over 24 hours representing normal operations. Third, a fundamental structural mismatch exists where the volume heuristic captures "chattiness" or total activity levels, while FastRP embeddings encode positional properties such as betweenness centrality and community structure, representing orthogonal signals that do not correlate. Multiple failed alternatives were attempted including percentile-based thresholds above the 95th percentile of subnet activity that still captured benign infrastructure, connection diversity metrics based on unique ports and services that correlated with legitimate servers including web and mail systems, and temporal windowing approaches requiring more than X edges in less than Y minutes that demanded hand-tuned parameters specific to each subnet. The lesson learned is that volume-based heuristics are fundamentally insufficient for pivot identification without ground truth calibration, as structural embeddings provide qualitatively different information than activity volume, explaining the observed negative correlation. Future label-agnostic work requires one of three approaches: semi-supervised learning using small labeled seed sets of 10-20 known pivots to calibrate per-subnet baselines, ensemble feature models combining FastRP with Burst Score and Connection Velocity given that temporal features maintained positive discrimination with d equals 1.04, or outlier detection approaches modeling normal behavior per subnet and flagging deviations though requiring 4-6 weeks of baseline data per subnet.
The subnet-level granularity exhibited by FastRP embeddings raises the question of whether this characteristic represents a desirable feature or problematic limitation. The observation that only 14 unique FastRP similarity values emerge across 21 subnets and 57,384 total reconnaissance windows indicates embeddings cluster at /24 subnet level rather than individual IP level. FastRP aggregates neighborhood information through iterative propagation, and with 4 propagation iterations combined with subnet-based connectivity patterns where most traffic occurs within or between subnets rather than randomly across the network, embeddings naturally converge to subnet-level representations. This characteristic provides several benefits including reduced overfitting through fewer degrees of freedom where 14 distinct values rather than 57,384 prevents memorization of specific samples, operational alignment because Security Operations Center analysts triage and contain threats at subnet boundaries rather than individual IP addresses, and improved robustness as subnet embeddings remain more stable than IP-level embeddings when network topology evolves through device additions or removals. However, limitations include loss of granularity where the system cannot distinguish between different IP addresses within the same subnet, and the subnet homogeneity assumption breaks down if a subnet contains heterogeneous hosts such as a /24 block mixing user workstations with server infrastructure. For this dataset with 21 subnets and 357 IP addresses, subnet-level granularity is appropriate given the small scale, but for larger networks with over 1000 subnets, IP-level resolution may become necessary to capture meaningful distinctions. The lesson learned is that granularity should match operational requirements and dataset scale, with the current approach providing the right abstraction level for this network size.
The architectural decision to employ Polars for multi-hop chain construction rather than pure Cypher queries reflects pragmatic recognition of system-specific strengths and limitations. The hybrid approach exports edges via Neo4j's APOC library then performs multi-hop joins in Polars rather than executing pure Cypher queries within the graph database. This design addresses memory constraints where Cypher `MATCH` queries for 4+ hop patterns exhaust Neo4j heap memory even with allocations tested up to 32 gigabytes, typically failing around 3-hop chains due to intermediate result materialization that cannot be streamed. Polars' `LazyFrame` with streaming execution enables processing datasets larger than available RAM through query optimization and chunked processing, avoiding the hard memory ceiling that limits Cypher. Additionally, Polars DataFrames support complex temporal constraints such as `hop2_ts > hop1_ts AND hop2_ts - hop1_ts < 86400` more expressively than Cypher `WHERE` clauses, enabling clearer specification of temporal causality requirements. The trade-off is that the hybrid architecture increases complexity through coordinated use of two processing engines but eliminates sampling constraints that would otherwise bias results. Pure Cypher approaches would require sampling to less than 1 million chains, systematically biasing results toward shorter chains that fit in memory while missing longer chains representing sustained campaigns. The lesson learned is that specialized systems excel in their native domains with Neo4j optimized for graph traversal and Polars designed for analytical joins, and hybrid architectures playing to each system's strengths can outperform monolithic approaches attempting to force all operations into a single system. An unexpected benefit emerged where Polars chain CSVs enable post-hoc analysis without re-querying Neo4j, as demonstrated by the tactic distribution analysis across hop depths reported in Table 4.1 that required simple CSV aggregation rather than complex graph queries, facilitating rapid exploration of alternative analytical perspectives without re-executing the expensive chain extraction pipeline.

## **Chapter VI: Conclusion and Future Work**
This work has developed a scalable graph-native framework for characterizing structural patterns in lateral movement pivots within APT campaigns using Neo4j and FastRP embeddings. The research demonstrates meaningful statistical evidence for structural differentiation while explicitly acknowledging methodological constraints that prevent claims of operational predictive capability. The contributions span infrastructure development, statistical validation, analytical capabilities, operational translation, and transparent documentation of both successes and limitations.
The core infrastructure contribution is a fully automated pipeline implemented in the `CART/` module that orchestrates end-to-end analysis from raw telemetry to publication-ready visualizations. This system ingests 1.9 million Zeek telemetry edges with MITRE ATT&CK labels into Neo4j, generates FastRP embeddings with configurable parameters including 128 dimensions and 4 propagation iterations, computes comprehensive baseline metrics spanning 9 comparison methods such as PageRank, Betweenness centrality, Burst Score, and Connection Velocity, and produces synchronized CSV outputs with statistical reports and high-resolution visualizations. The pipeline supports both label-aware mode for retrospective analysis with ground truth and label-agnostic mode for practical deployment scenarios, though the latter demonstrated significant limitations as documented in Section 4.3.
Statistical validation provides quantitative evidence that reconnaissance windows transitioning to pivots exhibit measurably distinct FastRP embeddings compared to non-pivot windows. The analysis reveals a Cohen's d effect size of 0.588 classified as medium magnitude with statistical significance of p < 1e-271, a mean similarity difference of 0.148 where pivots average 0.390 and non-pivots average 0.242, subnet-level clustering that produces only 14 unique similarity values across 21 subnets indicating embeddings aggregate at network segment boundaries, and Bonferroni-corrected significance testing where all p-values survive multiple comparison correction at the adjusted threshold of α = 0.0056. These results demonstrate that structural signals exist and are quantifiable, though the moderate effect size and substantial distribution overlap indicate that structure alone provides necessary but insufficient information for deterministic classification.
The scalable multi-hop chain analytics capability overcomes Neo4j's memory limitations through a hybrid architecture employing Polars for memory-efficient streaming joins. This system analyzes 668 million 2-hop chains without sampling, characterizes exponential decay across chain lengths including 198 million 3-hop chains, 45 million 4-hop chains, descending to 4,500 8-hop chains, quantifies adversary dwell time showing median intervals of 0.41 hours for first pivot and 40.65 hours for second pivot, and enables complete chain space materialization on commodity hardware through lazy evaluation and disk-based streaming. This capability provides unprecedented depth for kill chain characterization compared to prior work that typically samples or limits analysis to 2-hop patterns.
Translation into operational frameworks provides concrete SOC integration guidance despite the acknowledged need for further validation. The proposed workflows include risk-tiered alert prioritization with response time targets of under 15 minutes for CRITICAL alerts, under 1 hour for HIGH alerts, under 4 hours for MEDIUM alerts, and under 24 hours for LOW alerts, subnet-level containment strategies that leverage identified /24 bridge blocks connecting multiple VLANs for rapid isolation, calibration procedures for managing false positive rates through threshold tuning based on operational tolerance, and key performance indicator tracking for continuous model refinement including precision at top-K rankings and time-to-containment metrics. These frameworks require validation on representative operational data before deployment but provide actionable starting points for security operations integration.
The commitment to transparent methodological documentation distinguishes this work from typical machine learning security research that reports only positive results. Comprehensive reporting includes all experimental parameters such as window sizes, embedding dimensions, and filtering approaches; statistical tests with multiple comparison correction using Bonferroni adjustment; critical limitations including partial temporal leakage affecting 50% of test samples due to median-based filtering and extreme dataset class imbalance with 94.85% pivot rate inverting operational reality; absence of supervised machine learning baselines preventing assessment of whether observed performance represents fundamental limits of structural signals or merely unsupervised method limitations; negative results such as label-agnostic mode failure with Cohen's d of -0.32 demonstrating volume-based heuristics prove insufficient for pivot identification; and balanced accuracy of 0.50 equivalent to random guessing on balanced datasets, indicating limited operational utility without complementary temporal or behavioral features.

### **VI.A Hypothesis Evaluation**

The first hypothesis posited that reconnaissance victims subsequently transitioning into pivot nodes would exhibit higher cosine similarity to a historical pivot prototype embedding than those remaining dormant. This hypothesis is supported with significant caveats requiring acknowledgment. Evidence demonstrates that mean similarity for pivots at 0.390 significantly exceeds non-pivots at 0.242, with Welch's t-statistic of 39.33 and p-value below 1e-271 surviving Bonferroni correction at adjusted threshold α = 0.0056. The effect size Cohen's d of 0.588 classified as medium magnitude indicates practical measurable difference extending beyond mere statistical significance. The methodological context reveals that median-based temporal filtering provides perfect causality for the first 50% of samples chronologically while using contemporaneous network context for the remainder, making this effect size a weighted blend of pure prospective prediction estimated at d approximately 0.45-0.50 and retrospective characterization estimated at d approximately 0.65-0.70. The prototype is computed exclusively from training data maintaining evaluation integrity and preventing direct label leakage. Interpretation confirms that structural signatures exist and are quantifiable across both causal and contemporaneous evaluation regimes. While substantial overlap in distributions with pivot standard deviation of 0.336 and non-pivot standard deviation of 0.122 prevents deterministic classification, the consistent signal across temporal configurations validates that structural position correlates with pivot behavior in this dataset context.
The second hypothesis proposed that subnets prone to being used as pivot sources would display distinct structural telemetry compared to non-pivot subnets. This hypothesis is supported by case study evidence demonstrating clear differentiation. Quantitative evidence shows that high-risk subnet `143.88.11.0/24` exhibits mean FastRP similarity of 0.68 with 27,214 pivot instances, while dormant subnet `143.88.10.0/24` shows mean similarity of 0.09 with zero pivot instances despite experiencing 1,181 reconnaissance events. Structural interpretation reveals that pivot subnets function as bridge nodes connecting multiple VLANs, making them attractive targets for adversaries seeking lateral reach across network segments. The limitation exists that /24 aggregation masks within-subnet heterogeneity where a single compromised host does not necessarily make the entire 254-address block uniformly high-risk. However, the operational value of subnet-level risk assessment supports triage and containment strategies aligned with Security Operations Center workflows where analysts typically think in terms of network segments rather than individual hosts.
The third hypothesis claimed that structural separation between pivot and non-pivot groups would persist when evaluated across different temporal subsets of the dataset. This hypothesis is only partially tested, requiring additional work for complete validation. Evidence shows that the median-based filtering approach implicitly created two temporal subsets with the pre-median subset maintaining fully causal evaluation and the post-median subset incorporating partial temporal leakage. The fact that overall Cohen's d of 0.588 remained statistically significant across this combined evaluation suggests some temporal stability, as complete instability would have produced dramatically different effect sizes between subsets. However, explicit temporal subset analysis comparing the first 25% versus last 25% chronologically was not performed, preventing definitive claims about temporal consistency. Future work requires rigorous temporal cross-validation with train/test splits drawn from different time periods to validate whether structural patterns remain stable or exhibit drift as adversary behaviors and network topology evolve over extended campaigns.
Overall assessment indicates that hypotheses hold under the retrospective characterization framework established in this work but would require substantial methodological refinement to support prospective prediction claims. Per-sample temporal filtering ensuring each evaluation uses only strictly historical edges, proper train/test split reporting with held-out test set metrics, and balanced dataset evaluation with realistic pivot rates would be necessary prerequisites for operational prediction validation. Current evidence establishes that structural signals exist and are measurable through unsupervised embeddings, warranting future investment in rigorous predictive modeling with supervised machine learning methods and operational validation pilots.


### **VI.B Suggestions For Future Research**

Transitioning from retrospective characterization to validated prospective prediction systems requires systematic investigation across multiple research directions. The most critical priority is establishing rigorous experimental protocols with proper temporal train/test splits and held-out validation. Implementing 60/20/20 temporal splits where the model trains on the first 60% of data chronologically, validates hyperparameters on the next 20%, and reports final performance on the last 20% would ensure scientifically defensible results by computing pivot prototype embeddings exclusively on training data and evaluating all metrics on genuinely unseen test samples. Performance metrics may differ under stricter protocols with effect sizes potentially decreasing, but results would be publication-grade and eliminate concerns about train/test contamination that limit current findings. Per-sample temporal filtering where each reconnaissance event at time t uses only edges satisfying timestamp < t would enable true causal prediction claims by eliminating temporal leakage entirely. This approach requires iterative projection generation with one Neo4j graph projection per evaluation sample, but temporal leakage can be eliminated through efficient caching strategies, parallelization across compute clusters, and incremental graph update mechanisms. Effect sizes may be lower under strict per-sample causality with estimates around 0.45-0.55 compared to the current 0.588, but results would be scientifically unassailable. Balanced dataset evaluation through downsampling pivots to create 50/50 train and validation sets would provide realistic performance estimates for operational environments, with precision expected to decrease substantially from the current 0.949 to values more representative of 1% pivot rate scenarios encountered in production monitoring.
Supervised learning baselines represent another high-priority direction for establishing performance bounds and identifying informative signal sources. Training Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers using FastRP embeddings as features combined with temporal features such as Burst Score and Connection Velocity would establish supervised performance baselines revealing which signals contribute most to discrimination between pivots and non-pivots. These methods are expected to substantially outperform unsupervised similarity thresholding and require minimal computational resources for training and evaluation. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) or Graph Attention Networks (GAT) with 2-3 layers would enable state-of-the-art comparison by incorporating edge features including timestamps, service types, and ATT&CK labels into the learning process and comparing against FastRP combined with Random Forest ensembles. A pragmatic approach would establish Random Forest and XGBoost baselines first, with decision logic where performance below 0.70 AUC-ROC suggests structural signals may be too weak to warrant investment in complex graph neural network architectures, while performance exceeding 0.80 AUC-ROC would justify proceeding with GCN and GAT implementations requiring substantially more computational resources and hyperparameter tuning.
Operational validation through real-world deployment pilots would provide the ultimate test of practical utility by integrating structural risk scores into operational Security Operations Center workflows. Partnering with operational SOCs to deploy the system in production environments would enable tracking of critical metrics including precision at top-K rankings measuring what fraction of highest-scored alerts correspond to actual pivots, time to containment measuring how quickly confirmed pivots are isolated after initial alert, and analyst feedback capturing subjective assessments of operational value and false positive burden. Demonstrating or refuting operational value requires prerequisites including completion of supervised baselines establishing best achievable performance and per-sample temporal filtering ensuring causal prediction capability, without which deployment risks generating misleading or potentially harmful recommendations. Complementary live dataset collection through deploying Zeek sensors in enterprise networks with informed consent and institutional review board approval would enable collection of 3-6 months of telemetry with realistic pivot rates below 1%. This would address dataset bias concerns arising from the current 94.85% pivot rate and validate whether structural signals remain detectable at operational noise levels with orders of magnitude more benign traffic than the attack-focused UWF-ZeekData24 corpus.
Several methodological extensions merit consideration once baseline approaches are validated and operational feasibility is established. Temporal Graph Networks including TGAT and DySAT architectures could explicitly model time-evolving graph structure for comparison against static FastRP embeddings, revealing whether dynamic modeling provides sufficient performance gains to justify substantially longer training times estimated at 10-100 times longer than FastRP generation. This direction is contingent on GCN and GAT baselines showing strong performance exceeding 0.80 AUC-ROC, as weak baseline performance would indicate insufficient signal for complex temporal architectures to extract. Ablation studies systematically testing FastRP with varying dimensions including 64, 128, and 256, propagation iterations spanning 2, 4, and 8 iterations, and window configurations including 24h/12h, 48h/24h, and 72h/36h splits would assess sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and demonstrate whether findings are robust across configuration space or highly dependent on specific parameter selections. Semi-supervised and transfer learning approaches including weak supervision with confidence weighting, contrastive learning objectives, and pseudo-labeling strategies would address label-scarce scenarios common in operational environments and test whether models trained on one network topology can transfer to different architectures with minimal retraining. Transfer learning experiments would evaluate whether embeddings learned on the UWF network generalize to enterprise networks with different address allocation schemes, cloud environments with dynamic topology, or operational technology networks with distinct communication patterns

### **VI.C Closing Remarks**

This work represents a foundational step toward integrating graph-native structural analysis into operational security workflows for advanced persistent threat detection. By demonstrating that lateral movement pivots occupy measurably distinct structural positions within network graphs as evidenced by Cohen's d effect size of 0.588 and statistical significance of p < 1e-271, we provide quantitative evidence that network topology encodes information about adversary behavior extending beyond what is captured by traditional flow-based heuristics or signature-based detection methods. The structural perspective offers complementary signal that may prove valuable when combined with temporal and behavioral features in ensemble classification systems.
However, the path from retrospective characterization to prospective operational prediction requires substantial additional work addressing fundamental methodological constraints. The limitations acknowledged throughout this manuscript including aggregated train/test reporting preventing assessment of generalization to unseen data, partial temporal leakage affecting 50% of evaluated samples through median-based filtering, and inverted class distributions with 94.85% pivot rate creating artificially optimistic precision estimates are not minor technical details but fundamental constraints preventing operational deployment claims. These limitations must be systematically addressed through rigorous experimental protocols including held-out test set evaluation, per-sample temporal filtering, and balanced dataset validation before the research community can confidently deploy structural prediction systems in production security environments where false positives carry operational costs and false negatives carry security consequences.
The contributions of this work span three primary domains providing foundation for future research. First, a reproducible and scalable infrastructure integrates Neo4j graph database capabilities with Polars high-performance analytics through a hybrid architecture enabling comprehensive multi-hop chain analysis of 668 million 2-hop chains without sampling constraints that have limited traditional graph analysis approaches. Second, quantitative statistical evidence demonstrates that structural signals exist and are detectable through unsupervised FastRP embeddings with medium effect size, providing proof-of-concept evidence warranting investment in supervised machine learning methods and rigorous validation protocols on operational data. Third, an operational integration framework translates analytical findings into concrete Security Operations Center guidance including risk-tiered triage workflows, subnet-level containment strategies, and calibration procedures for managing false positive rates, even while explicitly acknowledging these frameworks require validation on representative operational datasets with realistic class distributions before deployment recommendations can be made with confidence.
The transparent documentation of both capabilities and limitations represents a commitment to scientific integrity that we hope will guide future research in graph-based threat detection. Explicit reporting includes negative results such as label-agnostic mode failure with Cohen's d of -0.32 demonstrating volume-based heuristics prove insufficient, methodological constraints including aggregated evaluation and temporal leakage limiting claims about predictive performance, and realistic performance expectations acknowledging precision estimates are optimistic by one to two orders of magnitude compared to operational environments with sub-1% pivot rates. This transparency enables the research community to build upon solid foundation understanding both what has been demonstrated and what remains to be validated.
As the cybersecurity community continues to grapple with sophisticated advanced persistent threat campaigns that evolve faster than signature-based defenses can adapt, structural graph analysis offers a complementary approach focusing on the fundamental network topology that adversaries must navigate during lateral movement phases. While this work does not provide a complete solution ready for operational deployment, it establishes that structural context is informative, quantifiable through modern graph embedding techniques, and worthy of continued investigation with more rigorous methodologies addressing the limitations documented throughout this manuscript. The combination of structural position, temporal dynamics, and behavioral indicators may ultimately provide the ensemble signal necessary for reliable pivot prediction in operational environments.
